## 1. Who we are and what we do

The Company operates an online platform that produces residential rental agreements for the
Indian market and takes them through to execution. A user completes a structured form
(parties, premises, term, rent, deposit, and a set of further commercial terms), and the
platform generates a completed agreement in PDF form. The Company's staff then purchase a
real e-stamp certificate from the SHCIL portal out of band and affix it to the instrument.
The executed copy is signed remotely by both parties using Aadhaar-based electronic
signature under section 3A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, through a licensed
e-sign service provider. A signed PDF and an audit trail are returned to the user.

The user pays the Company a **single flat platform fee**. Statutory stamp duty is purchased
by the Company's staff and affixed on the user's behalf; the Company holds no franking
licence or authorisation of its own and buys the certificate through the ordinary SHCIL
channel.

**Present stage.** The platform is live but restricted to the founding team. There are **no
paying external customers**, the e-sign integration runs against the provider's test
environment, and no agreement generated by the platform has been relied upon by a member of
the public. We are writing to you before that changes.

**No human drafting.** Every agreement is produced from a single stored template. There is
no advocate in the loop and no per-customer drafting. The wording reproduced in this brief
is therefore the wording that every customer receives; a defect in it is a defect in every
document we have ever issued.

**One control worth knowing about.** For every agreement generated, we permanently record a
cryptographic fingerprint of the exact template wording used to produce it, together with
the version of each component. For any document we have ever issued we can state precisely
what its template said on the day it was generated, and identify every other document
produced from the same wording. This matters to several of the questions below: if you tell
us a clause is wrong, we can identify exactly which agreements carry it.

## Question 1 — Is "Leave & Licence" the correct characterisation of this instrument?

**This is the question we would most like answered, and it is logically prior to the other
four.**

**What we do now.** The instrument is titled and recited as a leave and licence
arrangement. The subtitle reads "Residential Tenancy (Leave & Licence)", and the opening
recital reads:

> "This Residential Rental Agreement (Leave and Licence) is made between [Owner name], son /
> daughter / spouse of [Owner's father's or spouse's name], residing at [Owner's address]
> (the 'Owner'), and [Tenant name], son / daughter / spouse of [Tenant's father's or
> spouse's name], residing at [Tenant's address] (the 'Tenant')."

**Why.** It follows the form in common commercial use for residential arrangements of this
kind, and it is the characterisation the market appears to adopt.

**Our concern, offered as an observation and not as a conclusion.** Several features of the
instrument appear to us to sit uneasily with a pure licence characterisation, and we would
rather raise them than have you assume we had considered them:

(a) the instrument is characterised as a **licence** in its title and recital, but uses
**"Owner" and "Tenant"** as its defined terms throughout, rather than licensor and licensee;

(b) the operative wording is in the language of a demise — *"The Owner **lets** to the
Tenant the residential premises…"*, *"This **tenancy** is granted for a term of…"*;

(c) the Telangana version expressly invokes a **tenancy statute** — the Telangana Buildings
(Lease, Rent and Eviction) Control Act, 1960 — as its governing law, and imports from it a
protection against disconnection of essential services (Annexure B, clauses 1 and 4). We
are conscious that a licence would not ordinarily attract that Act;

(d) the term defaults to **eleven months**, and both stamp/registration clauses are drafted
so that compulsory registration is triggered only *where the term exceeds eleven months*.

**What we are asking.** Is "Leave & Licence" the correct characterisation for what this
instrument actually does? If it is not — or if the instrument as drafted in fact creates a
lease notwithstanding its label — please tell us (i) what the correct characterisation is,
(ii) what consequences follow for the stamp-duty basis and the registration trigger, and
(iii) whether the defined terms and the operative language should change to match.

**What changes if you disagree.** The characterisation is carried in the title, the
subtitle and the recital of the **shared** template, so it is the same in every state.
Changing it is a single edit that takes effect everywhere at once — but by the same token,
if it is wrong it has been wrong everywhere. It is because of that reach that we are asking
before we have external customers rather than after.

## Question 2 — Must a Telangana residential agreement always carry the statutory addendum?

**What we do now.** A Telangana agreement always carries, and the user cannot remove, an
addendum containing (i) a governing-law clause invoking the Telangana Buildings (Lease, Rent
and Eviction) Control Act, 1960; (ii) a stamping and registration clause referring the
parties to the jurisdictional Sub-Registrar under the Registration Act, 1908, and allocating
the duty and charges; (iii) where a duty amount has been entered, a statement of the amount
paid; and (iv) a clause reflecting the protection against withholding or disconnection of
essential supplies. The full wording is at **Annexure B**.

The Telangana version also **replaces** the general stamp-and-registration covenant used
nationally (Annexure A, clause 24) with the Telangana-specific one at Annexure B, clause 2,
so that the instrument contains one such covenant and not two.

**Why.** Until 7 September 2026 this addendum was optional and the user had to select it.
Because the Telangana version had already displaced the general stamp-and-registration
covenant in favour of the Telangana one, a user who did not select the addendum received an
agreement containing **no stamping or registration clause at all** — a worse instrument than
the national default. We made the addendum mandatory the same day to close that gap, which
also restored the governing-law and essential-services clauses. No external customer was
affected: we have confirmed that only founding-team agreements were generated in the
affected period.

**What we are asking.** Is it right that these four clauses always appear in a Telangana
residential agreement, and is their wording correct? In particular, is the eleven-month
registration trigger in Annexure B, clause 2 correctly stated, and is it appropriate that
the allocation of duty and registration charges is left to the parties' selection?

**What changes if you disagree.** If you consider the addendum genuinely optional, we will
make it optional again **and** restore the general stamp-and-registration covenant to the
Telangana version in the same change, so that no version of the instrument can ever be
issued without one.

## Question 3 — Are the shared commercial terms genuinely pan-India, and are their
defaults appropriate?

**What we do now.** A group of commercial terms is held in the **shared** template and is
therefore offered in identical wording in every state, Telangana included. They fall into
two categories, and we would ask you to treat the first as the more important:

- **Terms that appear by default**, because the form pre-fills the underlying figure, so
  they are in practice terms of nearly every agreement we issue: a **six-month lock-in**
  (Annexure A, clause 4) and an **annual rent escalation of five per cent** (clause 7). A
  **one-month termination notice** period (clause 5) always appears.
- **Terms the user adds if they want them:** maintenance charges, utilities and
  late-payment charges (clauses 9 to 12); occupancy limits, pets and parking (clauses 14 to
  16); special conditions (clause 23); and a fixtures and inventory annexure (clause 29).

**Why.** We took the view that these are ordinary commercial terms of a residential letting
and carry no state-specific legal content, so that the only genuinely state-specific
material is the statutory addendum at Question 2.

**What we are asking.** Is that right? Is any of these terms in fact regulated, restricted
or of different effect in a particular state — we are thinking especially of the lock-in
period, late-payment charges, the interest-free treatment of the security deposit (Annexure
A, clause 8), and any statutory cap on deposit or escalation — such that it should not be
offered in identical terms nationally?

**A second and separate question on the same clauses.** Quite apart from whether they are
state-specific: are the **pre-filled defaults themselves** appropriate? A six-month lock-in
and a five per cent annual escalation appear in an agreement unless the user actively
changes them. If either is unusual, one-sided, or liable to be read as unconscionable in a
residential letting, we would rather change the default than defend it.

**What changes if you disagree.** Any clause you identify as state-specific moves out of the
shared wording and into a state addendum of the kind described at Question 2. That is a
contained change per clause.

## Question 4 — Is the recital and the jurisdiction covenant correctly placed?

**What we do now.** The recital reproduced at Question 1, and the dispute and jurisdiction
covenant, both sit in the **operative covenants section** of the agreement and therefore
always appear, whatever else the user selects. The jurisdiction covenant takes one of two
forms depending on the user's choice:

> "Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement shall be subject to the
> exclusive jurisdiction of the courts at [city]."

or, where the user selects arbitration or mediation:

> "Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement shall first be referred
> to [arbitration / mediation]; subject thereto, the courts at [city] shall have
> jurisdiction."

In a Telangana agreement, the city defaults to **Hyderabad**; elsewhere the user supplies
it.

**Why.** A recital identifying the parties and a jurisdiction covenant seemed to us the sort
of provision that should not be capable of being omitted by a user who simply does not
select it.

**What we are asking.** Is that placement correct, and is either form of the jurisdiction
covenant objectionable? We would particularly welcome your view on whether the second form
is an adequate arbitration agreement, or whether — if a user selects arbitration — a
properly constituted arbitration clause (seat, number of arbitrators, appointment
mechanism, and the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996) is required. We suspect the
present wording is thin.

**What changes if you disagree.** Moving either provision, or replacing the arbitration
wording with a properly drafted clause, is a contained change to the shared wording.

## Question 6 — Please review and complete our draft terms of service, and advise on their scope

**What we do now.** We have a **draft**, reproduced in full at **Annexure C** and published on
the service at `agreementmitra.com/terms`. It is marked on its face as a draft pending your
review. We ask you to review it as a whole and to complete the sections it leaves open.

Two kinds of gap are marked in it, and the distinction matters to what we are asking of you:

- **FOR COUNSEL** — a section we have deliberately not written, because it is not ours to
  write. These are the ones we are asking you to draft: limitation of liability; our position
  on the stamp payment; the data-protection notice; and governing law, jurisdiction and

- **AWAITING PRODUCT INPUT** — a commercial term the company has not itself decided yet. **One

  have left it empty rather than filling it with a plausible figure, precisely so that you are

Two features of the drafted terms we would particularly like you to look at. First, **every
compensation we promise is a fixed sum** (INR 400 where we obtain a wrong certificate; INR 100
per working day to a maximum of INR 400 for a late stamping), and each is set at approximately
our own charge for the service. We deliberately did not express either as a share of the stamp
duty, because duty rises with the customer's rent while our charge does not. Please tell us
whether a limit of that kind is enforceable here, and whether the Consumer Protection Act, 2019
constrains it. Second, **these are promises we have imposed on
ourselves** rather than liabilities the general law would impose, and we are conscious from
Question 7 that an indemnity insurer may treat the two differently — we would welcome your view
on the interaction before we publish a final version.

**We have also moved the disclaimer.** When we first drafted this brief, the only "not legal
advice" notice anywhere on the service was a line at the foot of the FAQ on our marketing
page — our **marketing copy** was disclaimed and the product was not, which we were conscious
was exactly the wrong way round. The notice now appears on the screens where the customer
reviews the document, confirms contact details, and completes payment, and on the on-screen
document preview; each instance links to the terms. See (d) below for the one placement
question we have left open for you.

**Why.** The service has been in restricted use by the founding team only, so nothing has
depended on it. That protection expires the moment we admit an external customer.

**What we are asking you to cover.** Beyond the usual, we would draw your attention to four
features of the service that we think bear on scope:

(a) **We act on the user's behalf in a statutory payment.** The user pays us one flat fee.
Our staff then purchase the SHCIL e-stamp certificate and affix it. We hold no franking
licence. We would like your view on whether that makes us an agent for the purposes of the
stamp payment, what we should say about it, and what our position is if a certificate is
wrongly denominated, wrongly attributed, or rejected.

(b) **We handle Aadhaar-based e-signature through a third-party provider.** To be precise
about what we hold, because the answer may turn on it:

- The signer authenticates **on the provider's own page**, not through us. **No Aadhaar
  number, virtual ID or one-time password passes through our systems or is stored by us**,
  and we log none of it.
- We do collect and store, for each signer, the data needed to invite them and to identify
  them on the instrument: **name, email address and, optionally, telephone number**, together
  with the party details typed into the agreement (parentage and address).
- We store the **signed PDF** and the provider's **completion audit trail**. We keep the
  audit trail as an opaque file: we do not parse it or extract fields from it. We understand
  it to be the provider's own evidence of the authentication, and it may therefore contain
  identity evidence generated at the provider's end.
- We may ask the provider to **verify the signer's name against Aadhaar** as part of the
  signing flow. The comparison is performed by the provider; we receive its outcome.

We need to know what we must disclose about this, whether the Digital Personal Data
Protection Act, 2023 requires a separate privacy notice and a stated basis and retention
period rather than terms of service alone, and whether our retention of the provider's audit
trail carries obligations of its own.

(c) **The service is deliberately available without an account.** A user can generate,
pay for and sign an agreement without registering. We would like to know how acceptance of
the terms should be captured in that flow so as to be binding — and whether the absence of a
registration step is itself a problem.

(d) **We would like to know where the disclaimer should live.** Our own instinct is that a
"this is not legal advice" notice belongs in the terms and on the screens the user sees
before executing, and that printing such a notice **inside the executed instrument itself**
would be odd and might weaken the document. We would rather have your view than act on our
instinct.

We have now acted on the first half of that instinct: the notice is live on the on-screen
preview of the document and on the review, contact and payment screens. It is built so that
it renders **on screen only** and is suppressed whenever the document is printed or turned
into a PDF, so it appears in neither the executed instrument nor a draft PDF the customer
downloads. **The question we have left open is whether it should appear in the executed
document after all**, and it is a one-line change for us either way.

arrangement?

# Part D — The legal-advice boundary

## Question 8 — At what point does selecting clauses for a user become the practice of law?

**What we do now.** The user is shown a catalogue of optional clauses and chooses which to
include. The system supplies defaults, but the selection is presented as the user's. The
system does not ask about the user's circumstances and does not recommend anything.

**What we plan to build.** A rules engine that would take what it knows about the user —
their state, the nature of the premises, the term, the parties' answers to a small number of
questions — and **itself determine** which clauses their agreement should contain, on the
basis of the law of the relevant state. This is the core of our product proposition: that
the agreement is jurisdictionally correct without the user having to know what "correct"
means. It has not been built.

**What we are asking.**

(a) Where does each of those two products sit in relation to the restriction on practising
law in the Advocates Act, 1961 and the Bar Council of India rules — a **catalogue the user
selects from**, and a **system that selects for the user on the basis of their
circumstances**?

(b) If the second is problematic, does interposing a confirmation step cure it — the system
recommends and the user accepts — or is the recommendation itself the difficulty?

(c) Does it change the analysis if the rules themselves have been **reviewed and approved by
an advocate in advance**, so that what the system applies is settled professional judgment
rather than judgment formed at the moment of use?

(d) Are there structural arrangements — a partnership or panel arrangement with an advocate,
a defined referral point, particular framing — commonly used by Indian legal-technology
businesses to stay on the right side of this line?

**Why we ask now.** These are two different products with two different risk profiles, and
we have not yet committed to either. Choosing between them now costs us a design decision.
Discovering the answer after we have built the second would cost us the product. Of
everything in this brief, this is the question most likely to be expensive if it is left.

# Annexure A — Operative wording of the national agreement

# Annexure B — The Telangana addendum

A Telangana residential agreement is the whole of Annexure A, **except** that clause 24 of
Annexure A is omitted and the following section appears immediately before the execution
block. The user cannot remove it (see Question 2).

**Section heading: "Statutory (Telangana)"**

The section records the stamp duty paid, and which party bears the registration charges, and
then contains:

1. "This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of India and the tenancy laws applicable in
   the State of Telangana, including the Telangana Buildings (Lease, Rent and Eviction)
   Control Act, 1960, as amended."

2. "This Agreement shall be duly stamped and, where the term exceeds eleven (11) months,
   compulsorily registered before the jurisdictional Sub-Registrar in the State of Telangana
   under the Registration Act, 1908. The stamp duty and registration charges shall be borne
   by the [owner / tenant / shared]."

3. *(appears only where a duty amount has been entered)* "The stamp duty paid on this
   Agreement is INR [amount]."

4. "In accordance with the tenancy law applicable in Telangana, the Owner shall not withhold
